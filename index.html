Why food service, specifically

This is not a security product pointed at restaurants. The economics, the failure modes and the standards of food service are what make the category work at all.

The standards are already written down

Every chain has an operations manual specifying what a clean bar looks like, how long hands are washed, where the cloths go, how a drink is presented. Most industries need someone to define the standard before software can check it. Food service wrote the specification years ago.

Compliance is sampled, not measured

An auditor visits, scores the branch and leaves. Everyone knows when the visit is coming. What happens on the other twenty-nine days is invisible to the operator, and that is where the risk actually lives.

Turnover makes training perishable

Staff churn in this sector is high and continuous. A standard taught at induction decays within months, and nobody can see the decay until an incident or an inspection makes it visible.

Failures are expensive and visual

A hygiene incident is a brand event, not a store event. A slow drive-thru costs measurable revenue per car. Till shrink compounds quietly. All of it leaves visible evidence on cameras that already record and that nobody watches.

Thin margins reward small gains

Seconds of service time, a few points of waste, one avoided incident — each changes the shape of a store's month. The bar for a system paying for itself is lower here than in most sectors.

Multi-site is the entire point

A single café gains little; the owner is standing in it. A chain gains one comparable measurement across every branch, which is the first time an operations director can rank sites on something other than sales.

Cloud from end to end

Streams are pulled from each branch into our cloud, where decoding, inference, rules and evidence all live. Branches get no appliance, no GPU and no maintenance obligation. A software connector is the entire on-site footprint.

Nothing to install, nothing to service

A connector process on an existing machine in the branch, or a small gateway where none exists. No inference hardware means no site visits, no thermal failures, and no depreciating asset sitting in hundreds of stores.

Models improve everywhere at once

A retrained model deploys centrally and every branch benefits the same hour. On distributed inference hardware the same improvement becomes a fleet upgrade campaign.

Bandwidth managed by design

We pull the low-resolution analytic sub-stream rather than the recording stream, open full resolution only when an event needs evidence, and schedule streams to trading hours and to the zones a use case actually needs.

We ship one product first: handwash verification

The whole pipeline is designed, but a research group that ships six things at once ships none of them. Hand hygiene goes first because it has a rare combination — an objective standard, a model that transfers between customers without retraining, and a buyer who already holds budget for compliance.

How it works

1

Mark the sink, enter the standard

The sink area is drawn once per branch and the chain's own rule is configured: minimum duration, soap required, and which tasks must be followed by a wash.

2

Stream the zone, not the store

Only the camera covering the sink is pulled, at analytic resolution, during trading hours. One zone per branch is what keeps the first product's cloud cost predictable.

3

Detect the person, then the hands

Someone entering the sink zone triggers pose estimation focused on hand and forearm keypoints. That is what makes duration and motion measurable, rather than merely recording that a person stood at a sink.

4

Classify the wash sequence

A temporal model scores the sequence — approach, water, soap, scrub duration, rinse. A short wash and a rinse without soap are different outcomes, not one undifferentiated failure.

5

Score against the chain's rule

The event is compared to the configured standard and written as a compliant or non-compliant record with duration, station and time. Never as an identified individual.

6

Report as a pattern

Managers see compliance rates by shift, station and branch, with example clips. Nothing accuses a person in the moment, which is what keeps a system like this in use past week two.

Technology, and why this technology

Person detection and tracking

Modern single-stage detectors are fast enough to run many streams per GPU and accurate enough on people that no customer-specific training is required. It is the cheapest possible trigger for everything downstream.

Keypoint pose estimation

Chosen over plain object detection because the question is what the hands are doing over time, not whether a person is present. Pose gives measurable geometry — hands together, at the tap, in motion — from a camera never installed for this purpose.

Temporal action recognition

A wash is a sequence, not a frame. Frame-level classification cannot separate a real wash from a two-second rinse, and that distinction is the entire product.

Zone and rule engine

Keeps the model general and the policy local. One trained model serves every customer while each chain configures its own duration, soap rule and trigger tasks without anyone retraining anything.

Event store with clip references

Events carry a timestamp pointer and clips are kept only for exceptions and audits. Storage stays proportionate to what is actually reviewed, and footage stays out of the system by default.

Pooled cloud inference

One zone per branch is a small, schedulable workload — the shape that suits pooled GPU capacity. It also means the first customer and the fiftieth run identical code.

The pipeline behind it

Each capability below is researched, scoped and sequenced, and they share engines deliberately — a new product is mostly new rules and new labelled examples rather than a new research programme. Open any one for its workflow and its technology rationale.

Glove and bare-hand compliance

Next after handwash+

What it does

Flags ungloved hands during tasks that require gloves, and direct hand contact with ready-to-eat items in defined preparation areas.

Workflow

  1. Mark preparation zones and the tasks that require gloves.
  2. Detect hands entering the zone and classify glove state.
  3. Associate the hands with the item or surface being touched.
  4. Score against the chain's glove policy and log the event.
  5. Report compliance rate by station and shift.

Technology, and why

  • Hand detection and segmentation — reuses the pose engine built for handwash, so the marginal research cost of this product is small.
  • Glove-state classification — a narrow classifier is far more reliable than a general detector here, and accuracy improves sharply when the chain uses coloured gloves. We recommend that process change openly rather than hiding the dependency.
  • Zone rules — glove policy differs by task and by chain, so it belongs in configuration, not in the model.

Drive-thru service time

Operations line+

What it does

Times every vehicle across lane zones — arrival, order point, window, departure — and warns the branch before a car breaches the service target rather than after.

Workflow

  1. Define lane zones on the drive-thru camera view.
  2. Detect and track each vehicle through the zones.
  3. Timestamp transitions and compute per-segment times.
  4. Warn in-branch as a car approaches the threshold.
  5. Report averages, peaks and distribution by daypart.

Technology, and why

  • Vehicle detection with multi-object tracking — tracking rather than counting, because the metric is per-car duration and cars queue closely together.
  • Zone transition logic — replaces buried loop sensors, which is what makes this deployable without civil works.
  • Timer integration where one exists — where a chain already runs a drive-thru timer we consume its timestamps instead of publishing a competing number. Arguing with the clock on their own wall is a losing position.

Till exception review

Loss prevention line+

What it does

Surfaces refunds, voids, discounts and no-sale drawer openings that happened with no customer at the counter, each linked to the matching video.

Workflow

  1. Connect the chain's transaction feed.
  2. Detect customer presence at the counter continuously.
  3. Join each flagged transaction to the presence state in its window.
  4. Retain a clip for exceptions only.
  5. Rank patterns by terminal, shift and role.

Technology, and why

  • Transaction event integration — the detection genuinely lives in the transaction log rather than the video. Claiming otherwise produces a weaker and more expensive product.
  • Customer presence detection — deliberately the simplest model in the catalog, because the only visual question is whether anyone was there.
  • Time synchronisation discipline — drift between till and video silently corrupts every correlation, so it is treated as a precondition rather than a detail.

Spill and floor hazard

Safety line+

What it does

Detects liquids, dropped items and obstructions in customer and working areas, and escalates when they remain past a set time.

Workflow

  1. Define floor zones and dwell thresholds.
  2. Compare the current floor state against its learned normal.
  3. Ignore transient objects and people passing through.
  4. Alert when an anomaly persists, and again if it stays uncleared.

Technology, and why

  • Background and anomaly modelling — spills have no fixed appearance, so anomaly detection generalises where a trained object class would not.
  • Dwell thresholds — the difference between a hazard and a person standing still is time, which is a rule rather than a model.
  • Per-surface calibration — reflective and patterned floors are the known failure mode, so calibration is part of onboarding rather than a hidden risk.

Pickup shelf and order staging

Delivery line+

What it does

Tracks how long prepared orders sit on the collection shelf and flags orders going cold or standing unclaimed.

Workflow

  1. Define shelf positions on the pickup camera.
  2. Detect placement and removal per position.
  3. Time each order's stay on the shelf.
  4. Alert on breach and report staging times by daypart.

Technology, and why

  • Fixed-position presence detection — shelf positions do not move, making this among the most reliable detections available and a strong early revenue product.
  • State timing — the value is entirely in duration, so the model only has to be right about presence, not about contents.
  • Optional order-system link — connecting the order feed turns a dwell metric into a named-order alert without changing the vision layer at all.

Camera health and tamper

Runs from day one+

What it does

Detects cameras that have stopped streaming, been covered, knocked out of position or lost focus, across the whole estate, with no configuration.

Workflow

  1. Connect streams and store a reference frame per camera.
  2. Monitor availability and compare scene structure continuously.
  3. Alert on outage, obstruction, defocus or field-of-view change.
  4. Re-baseline after legitimate maintenance.

Technology, and why

  • Stream availability monitoring — needs no model at all, and typically reveals how many cameras in a large estate have already quietly failed.
  • Scene comparison — brand-independent, which matters on estates assembled over a decade from several vendors.
  • Zero configuration — the only capability that works the hour it is connected, which makes it the natural opening of every commercial conversation.

What funding builds

The research is done and the sequence is set. Capital converts a mapped pipeline into shipped products — one squad per capability, added only as the preceding one reaches production.

Vision engineer

Owns one capability's model end to end: training, evaluation, and the accuracy the product commits to.

Data and annotation

Builds and maintains the labelled sets. This is the asset that compounds, and the constraint that sets timelines.

Integration engineer

Connects cameras, recorders and transaction systems. Each integration is written once and reused across every customer on that stack.

Deployment and success

Onboards branches, tunes thresholds and keeps alert volume honest — the difference between a system in daily use and one quietly ignored.

The first squad ships hand hygiene to production with a pilot chain. The second is added only once the first is live, on whichever capability the pilot's own data shows is most wanted. Sequencing this way ties research cost to shipped revenue rather than to a roadmap.
